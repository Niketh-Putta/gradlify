### Draft 1: LinkedIn Shorts / Platform Change

LinkedIn is slowly becoming every app I tried to escape.

First it was TikTok.
Then Instagram.
Then YouTube.

Now LinkedIn wants short-form dopamine too.

And I get why.
Short videos keep people scrolling.
More scrolling means more attention.
More attention means more money.

But the reason I started posting here was because LinkedIn felt different.

It was one of the only platforms where a 16-year-old building an education startup could post an idea,
and actual founders, students, teachers, and investors would reply thoughtfully.

That is rare.

I don’t want LinkedIn to become another place where the loudest clip wins.

I want it to stay a place where the best ideas can still breathe.

Maybe I’m being dramatic.

But if every platform becomes the same,
where are builders supposed to think in public?

Would you actually use LinkedIn shorts?

### Draft 2: Education Access

The most unfair part of education isn’t exams.

It’s information.

Some students know about every competition, scholarship, research program, summer school, internship, and admissions trick before everyone else even knows those things exist.

And the crazy part is,
they’re not always smarter.

They’re just earlier.

Earlier to hear about it.
Earlier to apply.
Earlier to ask someone for help.

That gap compounds like mad.

By the time most students realise what opportunities are out there,
someone else has already built the profile,
sent the email,
joined the program,
and got the next opportunity from that one.

That’s the part that annoys me.

Talent is everywhere.
Access isn’t.

And if we actually care about merit,
we should care a lot more about who gets the information first.

What opportunity do you wish you knew about earlier?

### Draft 3: Gradlify Human Problem

A parent once messaged me because their child had no idea what to revise next.

Not because they weren’t trying.

They were doing worksheets.
Watching videos.
Going through random questions.

But every session felt like guessing.

And honestly, that’s how a lot of revision works.

Students don’t fail because they’re lazy.
They fail because they don’t know what the next highest-leverage thing is.

That’s the problem I keep coming back to with Gradlify.

Revision shouldn’t feel like throwing darts in the dark.

A student should be able to open one place and know:

- what they’re weak at
- what to practise next
- whether they’re actually improving

That sounds obvious.

But most students still don’t have it.

So we’re building it.

Slowly.
Messily.
But properly.

What was the hardest part of revising when you were in school?

### Draft 4: AI Study Tool Contrarian

Stop using AI to avoid thinking.

That is probably the biggest mistake students are making right now.

AI can explain a concept.
AI can mark an answer.
AI can generate questions.

But it cannot sit your exam for you.

At some point, you still need to look at a question and think.

No prompt.
No hints.
No chatbot holding your hand.

That’s why I think the future of AI in education isn’t just giving students answers faster.

It’s training them to think better.

The best AI study tools won’t replace effort.
They’ll make effort more targeted.

Less guessing.
Less wasting time.
More honest feedback.

Because if AI makes students dependent,
we’ve failed.

If AI makes students sharper,
that’s when it gets interesting.

Are students using AI properly yet?

### Draft 5: Teen Founder Credibility

Being a young founder is weird because people either overhype you or underestimate you.

There is almost no middle.

Some people hear “16-year-old founder” and act like you’ve already built the next unicorn.

Others hear it and immediately assume it’s a school project with a landing page.

Both are wrong.

Most of the time, it’s much less glamorous.

It’s fixing bugs after school.
Replying to users when you should probably be revising.
Trying to understand payments, databases, marketing, parents, students, and exams all at once.

And still feeling like you’re behind.

But maybe that’s the advantage.

You get more attempts.
You get to be bad earlier.
You get to learn things most people avoid until they’re forced to.

Starting young doesn’t make you better.

It just gives you more reps.

And reps compound.

Would you rather start earlier and fail more, or start later with more certainty?

### Draft 6: LinkedIn Growth Truth

The weirdest thing about growing on LinkedIn is that the numbers make you less certain, not more.

At 500 followers, I thought 1,000 would make me feel legit.

At 1,000, I thought 2,000 would make me feel like I knew what I was doing.

Now I’m realising the goalpost just moves.

You get more impressions,
then wonder why another post did better.

You get more comments,
then wonder why the right people didn’t see it.

You get more followers,
then realise attention is not the same as trust.

That’s the part nobody tells you.

Growth is useful.
But it doesn’t remove doubt.

If anything, it gives you more data to overthink.

So I’m trying to focus less on “did this go viral?”

And more on:

Did this make people think?
Did this bring the right people closer?
Did this sound like me?

Because if you lose your voice chasing reach,
what exactly are people following?

### Draft 7: Founder Chaos

The scariest founder moments are never the ones you planned for.

They’re the random ones.

The bug that appears right before users log in.
The payment that doesn’t work.
The parent who messages confused.
The leaderboard that suddenly looks wrong.

And for a few minutes,
your brain goes straight to:

“it’s over.”

But then you fix one thing.
Then another.
Then another.

And somehow the disaster becomes a checklist.

That’s probably one of the biggest things building Gradlify has taught me.

Panic makes problems feel bigger.
Action makes them smaller.

Not instantly.
Not magically.

But enough to keep moving.

I used to think founders were calm because they had everything under control.

Now I think they’re calm because they’ve survived enough things going wrong.

What’s the worst last-minute problem you’ve had to fix?

Handstands are annoying because you can’t fake balance.

You either hold it,
or you fall.

No motivational quote helps.
No productivity hack helps.
No “founder mindset” helps.

Your body just tells you the truth immediately.

That’s what I like about training.

It’s honest.

And honestly, building feels similar.

You can say you’re building.
You can update your headline.
You can post about the vision.

But eventually the product has to work.
Users have to come back.
The thing has to stand on its own.

Otherwise you fall.

The good news is falling is literally part of learning the skill.

You don’t learn balance by thinking about balance.
You learn by kicking up again.

Same with building.

### Draft 9: Student Opportunity Rant

I don’t think students need more motivation.

They need better maps.

Everyone keeps telling ambitious students:

work hard
be consistent
put yourself out there
take opportunities

Cool.

But where are the opportunities?

Which competitions matter?
Which programs are actually worth applying to?
Which cold emails work?
Which internships accept students?
Which projects make people take you seriously?

That information is scattered everywhere.

Some students get it from parents.
Some get it from schools.
Some get it from older friends.

Most just guess.

And guessing is expensive when you’re young.

Because every missed opportunity becomes another student’s unfair head start.

I think the next big education unlock is not more content.

It’s navigation.

Students don’t just need to study harder.
They need to know where to aim.

### Draft 10: AI Building Speed

I don’t think people understand how weird building has become.

A few years ago, if you had an app idea,
you needed to either code properly,
find a technical cofounder,
or pay someone.

Now a student can go from idea to working prototype in a weekend.

That sounds amazing.

But it also creates a new problem:

starting is no longer the hard part.

Sticking with the right thing is.

Because when you can build anything,
it becomes very easy to build everything.

New idea?
New app.

Another trend?
New landing page.

Something gets hard?
Start again.

AI made building faster.
But it did not make focus easier.

That’s probably the skill that matters most now.

Not “can you build?”

But “can you keep caring after the first version exists?”

The coolest part of hitting follower milestones is not the number.

It’s recognising names.

There are people who comment so often that I notice when they’re missing.

People who give advice.
People who challenge posts.
People who somehow support every random experiment.

That’s the part I didn’t expect when I started posting.

I thought LinkedIn growth would feel like a scoreboard.

More followers.
More impressions.
More comments.

But the better part is that it starts feeling like a room.

And slowly,
you begin to recognise who’s in it.

That makes posting feel very different.

Less like shouting into the internet.
More like building with people watching.

So if you’ve been reading, commenting, or even just quietly following along:

thank you.

We’re still early.

Because anyone can update a headline.
